# Writing prose that someone will read

The reader has only the page. They cannot ask what you meant. Most unclear
writing comes from unclear thinking, so the first work happens before the
first sentence.

## Before drafting

- **Who is reading, and what do they already know?** Write for a bright
  person who was not in the room. Too much assumed and they are lost; too
  little and they skim.
- **State the point in two sentences** to yourself. If you cannot, you do
  not know what you think yet. Work that out before writing.
- **Decide what they should do or know after reading.** Everything that
  does not serve that is a candidate for cutting.

## Drafting

- **Put the conclusion first.** Then the support, then the detail. A reader
  who stops after one paragraph should leave with the main thing.
- **Choose the specific claim.** "This matters for several reasons" says
  nothing. Name the reason. Give the number, the example, the name.
- **Plain words and literal statements.** If a figure of speech has to be
  translated back into the claim, write the claim.
- **Use one term for one thing.** Changing the word makes the reader wonder
  if you changed the subject.
- **Commit.** Hedge where there is real uncertainty, once, and say what the
  uncertainty is. Hedging every sentence hides which doubts are real.
- **Let the content pick the form.** Reasoning goes in paragraphs. Steps go
  in a numbered list. Comparisons go in a table. Bullets cut the "because"
  out of an argument.

## Revising

Read it as the reader, cold.

- Cut the opening that announces what you are about to say, and the ending
  that repeats what you said.
- Cut every sentence the reader would not miss.
- Find the sentence you are proudest of. It is often there for you, not
  for them.
- Check each paragraph does one job, and that the order is the order the
  reader needs, not the order you thought of things.
- Check every fact, name, number and quotation against its source.

For the patterns that mark text as machine-written, use the `stop-slop`
skill on the draft.

## Academic writing

State the claim, then the evidence, then the limits. Distinguish your
result from your interpretation of it. Match the verb to the evidence:
"shows", "suggests" and "is consistent with" are three different claims.
Cite for claims, not for decoration. While drafting, write [cite] in place
of a reference you have not opened; never fill one in from memory. Follow
the field's and the venue's conventions, including voice (methods sections
in biology are often passive); read two papers from the venue first.

## Writing in someone else's voice

Read their existing text before writing. Match sentence length, vocabulary
and how much they hedge. Edit lightly: fix what is wrong and leave what is
only different from how you would say it.

## Traps

- Polishing sentences in a draft whose structure is wrong.
- Length as a signal of effort. Shorter is harder and better.
- Summarising when the reader asked for a judgement.
- Rewriting a user's draft when they asked for comments.

## Done when

A reader who knows nothing of the conversation can follow it, the point is
in the first paragraph, and nothing in it is there to fill space.
