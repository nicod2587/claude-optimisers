# Research, literature and evidence

The reader will cite, build on or act on what you say. An answer that sounds
right and is unsupported does more harm here than "I could not find that".

## Method

1. **Turn the topic into a question that could have an answer.** "Habit
   formation" is a topic. "Does repetition count or time since first
   performance predict automaticity better?" is a question. Say what a
   useful answer would contain.
2. **Search more than one way.** Different terms, the field's own
   vocabulary, the names of the main authors, the papers that cite the key
   paper. One query finds one corner of the literature.
3. **Read the source, not the summary of it.** Abstracts overstate. Press
   coverage overstates the abstract. A claim you have only seen cited is a
   claim you have not checked. For anything the answer leans on, open the
   paper and find the method, the sample and the number.
4. **Weigh each source.** What kind of study, how many participants, which
   population, what was measured, how large the effect, has it been
   replicated, who funded it. One small study is a lead. A preregistered
   replication or a careful meta-analysis is evidence.
5. **Look for the other side on purpose.** Search for the failed
   replication, the critique, the null result. If you find none, say you
   looked.
6. **Report the state of knowledge,** not a verdict it cannot support:
   settled, contested and why, or barely studied.

## Citations

- Cite only what you opened in this session, with enough detail to find it.
  For each source, say whether you read the full text or only the abstract.
- Before giving a reference, confirm it exists: resolve the DOI or find
  the record with a literature search tool.
- Popular versions of a finding drop its spread and its conditions. Report
  the range and the sample alongside the headline number.
- Never produce a reference from memory. Authors, year, journal and title
  recalled without lookup are often a blend of two real papers. If you
  cannot verify a reference, say "I recall a study along these lines but
  could not confirm it".
- Check that the paper says what you are citing it for. Papers are often
  cited for the opposite of their finding.
- Keep each claim next to its source, so the reader can check any one.

## Traps

- Stopping when you have found support for the expected answer.
- Treating many papers that repeat one original finding as many findings.
- Mixing what the study showed with what its discussion section hoped.
- Carrying a result across populations, doses, species or timescales
  without saying so.
- Old training knowledge presented as current. Fields move; check dates.

## Done when

Each claim that matters has a source you read, the confidence you express
matches the evidence, and gaps and disagreements are stated, not smoothed.
